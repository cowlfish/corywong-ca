Every seller asks this question. The answer is in the data, not the feeling.

## How market value is determined

A home is worth what a ready, willing, and able buyer will pay for it under current market conditions. Everything else is an opinion until a buyer signs an offer.

The tool for estimating market value is a comparative market analysis, or CMA. A CMA looks at recent sales of similar properties in the same area. Similar means comparable square footage, lot size, bedroom count, condition, and location. A lender's appraisal uses the same methodology.

The CMA is not a ceiling or a floor on what your home will sell for. It is a starting point for pricing strategy.

## What moves the number

Some factors affect value significantly. Others matter less than sellers expect.

Location within the neighbourhood is the most significant variable. A two-block difference in Toronto sometimes separates a school zone commanding a premium from one without. Proximity to transit and parks affects demand. The specific street matters.

Lot size and configuration matter, particularly for detached homes. A 25-foot frontage sells differently than a 30-foot frontage in most Toronto markets. Corner lots, ravine lots, and irregular lots each bring their own pricing dynamics.

Condition is a real factor. Buyers and their agents notice deferred maintenance, dated mechanical systems, and layouts that do not function well. A clean, well-maintained home without significant deferred work sells closer to its full market value. A home with visible issues requires a discount to reflect the buyer's cost of remediation.

Bedroom and bathroom count matters relative to the neighbourhood standard. If comparable homes in your area offer four bedrooms and yours has three, that gap is reflected in the price.

Finish quality matters within limits. A high-end renovation in a neighbourhood of standard finishes does not recover full cost. Buyers set their reference point by what else is available at the price.

## What doesn't move the number

What you paid for the home does not determine market value. Renovation costs do not translate dollar-for-dollar to sale price. The assessed value on your property tax bill is not a market value estimate.

What matters is what comparable homes sold for recently, and how your home compares.

## List price strategy

The list price is a strategic decision, not simply a reflection of market value.

In competitive segments, sellers list at or below market value to invite multiple offers. In balanced markets, the listing price and market value are close and the sale negotiates from there. In softer segments, sellers list slightly above market value to leave room for negotiation.

Getting this call wrong costs money. A price too low in a market without multiple offers leaves money on the table. A price too high sits, signals motivation, and often produces a lower final number than the correct price would have.

## The cost of overpricing

Overpricing is the most common seller mistake in Toronto.

A home that launches at the wrong price sits. Every week it sits, buyers and agents form a negative impression. Price reductions signal motivation and reduce buyer urgency. By the time a seller adjusts, they often accept less than they would have with accurate pricing from the start.

The pattern is consistent: homes that sell in the first two weeks of listing close closer to asking than homes that sit and reduce.

## When to list

The best time to list depends more on your circumstances than on market conditions. That said, the fall market in Toronto typically runs from mid-September through late November. It is the second-strongest selling window of the year, after April and May.
